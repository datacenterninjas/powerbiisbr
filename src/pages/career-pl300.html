Turning the Final Project into a portfolio piece

StepWhat to actually do
1Publish your Final Project report to a named workspace in the Service — the same step from the Module 5 Assignment
2Choose how to share it (next section — this decision matters more than it looks)
3Write one LinkedIn/CV line, not a paragraph — see the template below
4Test it yourself: open the link cold, time how long it takes you to find the headline number
Publish to Web vs. a sharing link — read this before either one: Publish to Web makes a report visible to literally anyone on the internet with the link, embeddable anywhere, no login required — genuinely public, no access control of any kind. A sharing link or workspace app keeps it restricted to people you actually grant access to. Never Publish to Web a report built on this course's Banking, HR, or Hospital practice data — those datasets are fictional, but they're shaped exactly like real PII (names, salaries, credit scores, diagnoses), and the habit of checking before publishing is the actual skill, not the specific file. For a portfolio piece, build it on the Sales or Retail dataset, or on public data of your own choosing, and only then consider Publish to Web.

CV/LinkedIn line template:

"Built an interactive Power BI dashboard analyzing [dataset/business area] to surface [N] actionable insights, including [the single most interesting finding] — [link]."

Example: "Built an interactive Power BI dashboard analyzing 100 days of e-commerce sales to surface 3 actionable insights, including that Electronics drives nearly a third of revenue despite being only the third-largest category by order count — [link]."

What a recruiter actually checks in the first 30 seconds — before reading a single insight:

  • 1
    Does it load fast and look intentional — a real title, a consistent theme, not the default blue Power BI ships with?
  • 2
    Is there one number they can find in under 3 seconds — a KPI card doing its actual job?
  • 3
    Does clicking something (a slicer, a bar) visibly do something — proof it's a real interactive report, not a static screenshot pretending to be one?

Case-interview practice

Six questions in the style a business-analyst interview actually asks — not "what does DAX stand for," but "here's a situation, what do you do." Attempt an answer out loud before revealing the model one.

"Sales dropped 12% last quarter — using this dashboard, where do you look first and why?"

Model answer: Start broad, then narrow — check the trend line first (was it a cliff in one month, or a slow slide all quarter?), then slice by the biggest dimension (Region or Category) to find where the drop concentrated, rather than assuming it's uniform. A single region collapsing points to something local (a competitor, a stockout); a uniform drop across everything points to something systemic (pricing, seasonality, the whole market). Only after locating WHERE does "why" become answerable.

"A regional manager says their region's numbers 'feel wrong.' How do you investigate?"

Model answer: Don't defend the dashboard — investigate it. Check Model view first for a relationship or cardinality issue silently duplicating or dropping rows (the exact class of bug the Troubleshooting page covers). Then check whether a Report- or Page-level filter is scoping the data more narrowly than the manager expects. Only after ruling out both does "the manager's expectation is wrong, not the number" become the honest conclusion — and even then, ask what they expected and why before asserting it.

"24 hours, one raw CSV, a board-ready dashboard by morning. Your first 3 moves?"

Model answer: (1) Load it and immediately turn on Column quality/distribution/profile — know what's broken before touching anything. (2) Clean in Power Query, not by hand in Excel — reproducible steps matter even under deadline pressure, maybe more so, since there's no time to redo a hand-fix that turns out wrong. (3) Build the 1–2 KPI cards and 1 chart that answer the single question the board actually asked, and stop — a board meeting rewards one clear number over four half-finished ones.

"Total Sales is up 8% year over year, but Profit Margin is down 3 points. What story does that tell?"

Model answer: Growth funded by margin — revenue is up but it's costing more to generate each rupee of it, which usually means discounting, a shift toward lower-margin products/channels, or rising input costs. The follow-up question, not the answer, is what actually matters here: which of those three is it? That's a CALCULATE-filtered measure by Category or Channel away from being answerable, not a guess.

"Explain the difference between a Report and a Dashboard to a non-technical stakeholder, in one sentence."

Model answer: "A Report is the full multi-page workbook you can dig into; a Dashboard is the one-page highlight reel pinned from it for someone who just wants the headline numbers." Precision matters less here than not making the stakeholder feel talked down to — one plain sentence beats a technically-perfect paragraph in this exact scenario.

"A stakeholder wants 15 KPIs on one page. How do you push back, and what do you offer instead?"

Model answer: Don't just say no — ask which 3–4 numbers they'd check first thing every single morning, and build a page around those; the KPI Card lesson's own rule of thumb (4–6 cards max, "beyond that, nothing feels important anymore") is the actual argument, not a personal preference. Offer the other 11 metrics as a second page, or a Matrix/Table someone can dig into on demand — the stakeholder's real need (comprehensive access) and their stated request (one page) usually aren't actually the same thing once you ask.

DAX interview questions

Six more, specifically on DAX — the topic most likely to actually get asked in a Power BI analyst interview, and the one this course spends a whole track on for exactly that reason.

"Explain the difference between a measure and a calculated column — to a manager who's never opened Power BI."

Model answer: "A calculated column is like a formula you drag down an Excel column once — it's baked in, the same for every row, forever. A measure is more like a calculator that re-does the math every time you ask it a new question, using whatever's currently in front of it." No DAX syntax, no "filter context" jargon — the manager needs the mental model, not the implementation.

"Your dashboard's Total row doesn't equal what you get from adding up the visible rows by hand. Walk me through how you'd investigate."

Model answer: First question: is the measure a plain SUM, or a ratio/average? A Total row recomputes the measure fresh in its own filter context — it doesn't literally sum the rows above it. For a plain SUM that happens to agree with manual addition; for a margin % or average, it almost never does, and that's expected behavior, not a bug. Only after ruling that out would I actually suspect the DAX formula itself.

"Revenue is up 8%, but the CEO asks: is that real growth, or just a price increase? Which measures do you build?"

Model answer: Revenue alone can't answer this — I'd build Total Quantity alongside Total Sales (SUM of OrderQuantity vs. SUM of SalesAmount) and a derived Average Selling Price = DIVIDE(Total Sales, Total Quantity). If Quantity is flat or down while Sales is up 8%, that's price-driven, not demand-driven — two very different stories the CEO needs to hear apart, not blended into one revenue number.

"When would you reach for CALCULATE with ALL — in plain business terms, not DAX syntax?"

Model answer: Any time the business question is "what share of the WHOLE is this," not "what's this slice's own number." A regional manager's dashboard showing each region's % of total company revenue needs every row to divide by the SAME grand total, regardless of which region that row happens to be — that's ALL removing the region filter just for the denominator, while the numerator still respects it.

"A colleague says their DAX measure is 'too slow.' What are the first two things you'd check?"

Model answer: First, whether it's actually a measure or a calculated column doing a measure's job — a calculated column recomputes across the whole table on every refresh, not just when queried. Second, whether an iterator (SUMX, AVERAGEX) is running row-by-row over an entire large fact table where a plain SUM or AVERAGE would have worked just as well — iterators are for when the calculation genuinely varies row by row, not a default habit.

"Explain 'filter context' to a non-technical stakeholder — without using the words 'filter' or 'context.'"

Model answer: "Every number on this dashboard is really the same question, answered fresh depending on what you've clicked. Click 'West,' and every card recalculates for West. Click a bar in a chart, same thing happens for that bar's category. Nothing is precomputed and stored per click — it's all recalculated live, every time, for whatever you're currently looking at." Explaining the effect a stakeholder can SEE beats naming the mechanism they can't.
